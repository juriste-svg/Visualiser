Harvard Medical School · On The Brain · Summer 2019

Your brain on screens

Pediatrician Michael Rich of Boston Children's Hospital studies how kids use digital media. His big idea: the clock is the wrong thing to watch.

The usual question

How many hours on screens?

Rich's question

What are you doing on it, and what is your brain doing back?

Screens can help kids learn and find community. They can also crowd out sleep and creativity. Here are the four ideas in the article, each with something to play with.

Idea 1 · The brain is under construction

Use it or lose it

A growing brain keeps building new connections between neurons and prunes the ones that don't get used. Whatever a child spends time doing shapes which wires survive, and digital media is part of that.

Rich calls much of screen stimulation “impoverished” next to real life: flat, mostly visual, and repetitive. The real world hits every sense at once.

Idea 2 · Why it's hard to put down

Your feed is a slot machine

“Virtually all games and social media work on what's called a variable reward system.”MICHAEL RICH

You never know if the next refresh brings a pile of likes or nothing. That uncertainty is exactly how slot machines hook people, and it switches on the brain's reward system. In kids and teens, the self-control system that should hit the brakes is still being built.

Idea 3 · The bedtime glow

Blue light steals sleep

HMS researchers found that using blue-light screens like phones before bed can suppress melatonin, the hormone that tells your body it's night. Less melatonin means later, worse sleep. Sleep is when the brain stores what it learned that day.

“So even if they stay awake in algebra class, they may not remember what happened in class yesterday.”MICHAEL RICH

Idea 4 · The case for doing nothing

Boredom is where ideas come from

“Boredom is the space in which creativity and imagination happen.”MICHAEL RICH

A screen fills every empty moment. Rich wants kids to have time to let their minds wander, because that blank time is where the brain starts making its own stuff.

So what do you do?

Think diet, not ban

Rich argues for balance: a varied mix of online and offline experiences, the way a good diet mixes foods.

Ask “what,” not “how long”

Video-calling grandma and doom-scrolling are both screen time. They do very different things to the brain.

Feed every sense

Outdoors, building, sports, face-to-face time. Real life gives the brain richer input than a screen can.

Guard the night

Phones away before bed protects melatonin, sleep, and the memories made that day.

Leave empty space

Let kids be bored sometimes. It is where creativity and imagination get to work.
